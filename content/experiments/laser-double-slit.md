## The question

If light is made of particles, why does it make a stripy pattern when it passes through tiny gaps?

## Learn just enough first

- [ ] Watch a short video on wave–particle duality and the double-slit experiment
- [ ] Read the double-slit chapter of *Quantum: A Guide for the Perplexed*

## Kit

- [ ] A low-power red laser pointer (Class 2, under 1 mW, wavelength usually 650 nm — check the label)
- [ ] A single hair, taped across a card with a hole cut in it
- [ ] Kitchen foil and a craft knife, to scratch two very close slits
- [ ] A wall or white card 2–3 m away, and a tape measure

## My prediction

What pattern do I expect on the wall: one bright spot, two bright lines, or something else?

## Method

1. Shine the laser past the hair onto the far wall, in a darkened room. Look for a row of bright and dark spots.
2. Measure the distance from the hair to the wall, \(L\), and the spacing between neighbouring dark spots, \(\Delta y\).
3. Now scratch two parallel slits in foil, as close as you can (under 0.5 mm apart), and repeat.
4. Photograph each pattern with a ruler held next to it.

> **Safety:** Never look into the laser or point it at anyone. Watch out for reflections off shiny surfaces.

## Result

The fringe spacing links the wavelength \(\lambda\), the distance \(L\) and the gap or hair width \(d\):

$$ \Delta y = \frac{\lambda L}{d} \quad\Rightarrow\quad d = \frac{\lambda L}{\Delta y} $$

Use it to work out the thickness of my hair (human hair is usually 50–100 micrometres) and the gap between my two slits.

## Explain it to a friend

Light going through two gaps spreads out and overlaps with itself. Where the waves line up you get bright stripes; where they cancel, darkness. The strange quantum part: even when light is sent one photon at a time, the stripes still build up — each photon seems to go through both slits.

## New questions

- What happens to the pattern if I move the wall further away?
- Why does a single hair make a pattern at all?
