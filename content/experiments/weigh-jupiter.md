## The question

Can I work out how heavy Jupiter is, using nothing but binoculars, a notebook and Kepler's laws?

## Learn just enough first

- [ ] Read about Kepler's third law: how a moon's orbit size and period depend on the planet's mass
- [ ] Use [Stellarium](https://stellarium.org) to find Jupiter and learn which moon is which

## Kit

- [ ] Binoculars (10x50 is ideal), steadied on a tripod, fence post or car roof
- [ ] A notebook with a pre-drawn line for each night
- [ ] Stellarium, to check identifications on the same night

## My prediction

The accepted mass is about \(1.9 \times 10^{27}\) kg — over 300 Earths. How close will a hobbyist get?

## Method

1. On each clear night, sketch Jupiter as a dot and mark each moon's position. Estimate distances in Jupiter-widths.
2. Note the date and time every time. Aim for as many nights as possible over 2–4 weeks — weekend nights are fine.
3. Concentrate on **Ganymede** (orbits in about 7 days) or **Callisto** (about 17 days).
4. Plot each moon's distance from Jupiter against time. It traces out a wave: the peak is the orbit radius \(a\), and the time for one full wave is the period \(T\).
5. Convert Jupiter-widths to metres using Jupiter's diameter, about 143,000 km.

> **Tip:** UK winters are cloudy. If gaps appear, use Stellarium to fill missing nights — note in the data which points are observed and which are simulated.

## Result

Kepler's third law, rearranged for the planet's mass:

$$ M = \frac{4\pi^2 a^3}{G\,T^2} $$

with \(G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\) (SI units), \(a\) in metres and \(T\) in seconds. As a check, Ganymede's real orbit (\(a \approx 1.07 \times 10^{9}\) m, \(T \approx 7.15\) days) gives about \(1.9 \times 10^{27}\) kg.

## Explain it to a friend

A heavier planet pulls its moons round faster. Measure how far out a moon orbits and how long it takes, and gravity tells you the planet's mass. It's the same method used to weigh the Sun, black holes and whole galaxies.

## New questions

- Do two different moons give the same mass?
- How does an error in the orbit size affect the answer? (Hint: it's cubed.)
